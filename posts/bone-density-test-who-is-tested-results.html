Bone Density Tests: Who Gets Tested and What the Results Mean

November 18, 2026

Osteoporosis is called a "silent" disease because, according to the National Institute of Arthritis and Musculoskeletal and Skin Diseases (NIAMS), you typically do not have symptoms. Fractures happen most often in the hip, the vertebrae of the spine, and the wrist, and the condition often goes unnoticed until a bone breaks. A bone density test is how doctors find out where your bones stand before that happens.

What a bone density test measures

NIAMS explains that a bone mineral density (BMD) test measures calcium and other minerals in bone, and that denser bones tend to be stronger and less likely to break. The most common version is a central DXA (or DEXA) scan, which uses radiation to measure the minerals in a specific area of bone, usually the hip and spine. In its diagnosis guide, NIAMS says DXA at the hip and spine is generally considered the most reliable way to diagnose osteoporosis and predict fracture risk.

MedlinePlus, from the National Library of Medicine, says the scan is painless and uses a slight amount of radiation, less than a chest x-ray. It adds that most experts consider the risk very low compared with the benefit of detecting osteoporosis before a fracture.

Who should be tested

The US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF), in a January 2025 recommendation, advises screening for osteoporosis in two groups of women:

Women who are 65 or older.

Postmenopausal women younger than 65 who are at increased risk of a fracture, as estimated by a clinical risk assessment. The USPSTF lists menopausal status, low body weight, a parent who had a hip fracture, cigarette smoking and excess alcohol among the risk factors to check.

For men, the sources differ. The USPSTF says the current evidence is insufficient to weigh the benefits and harms of screening, and that this is not a recommendation against it. MedlinePlus says experts disagree on testing men. The Johns Hopkins Medicine osteoporosis page recommends bone density scans for all women 65 and older, all men 70 and older, and certain younger adults based on risk factors or a prior fracture.

MedlinePlus also says younger women and men of any age may need testing if they have risk factors such as a fracture after age 50, a strong family history of osteoporosis, or long-term use of corticosteroids.

What the results mean

Results come as a T-score or a Z-score. NIAMS says the T-score is used for postmenopausal women and men age 50 or older, while the Z-score is used for premenopausal women, men younger than 50, and children. The lower your T-score, the higher your risk of a bone fracture.

MedlinePlus gives the T-score ranges. A score of -1.0 or above is normal. A score from -1.1 to -2.4 suggests low bone mass, called osteopenia. A score of -2.5 or below suggests osteoporosis. The test does not diagnose fractures. It helps estimate your future fracture risk, and treatment decisions depend on your overall risk, which is often measured with a tool called FRAX. SFL's article on osteoporosis versus osteopenia walks through the difference.

Before the test

MedlinePlus says to skip calcium supplements for 24 hours before the scan, remove metal items such as jewelry and belt buckles, and tell your provider if you are or could be pregnant.

How often to repeat the test is less settled. MedlinePlus does not give a schedule, and the USPSTF says the evidence on screening intervals is limited. Ask your provider what makes sense for you.

Steps you can take for bone health

NIAMS says calcium and vitamin D are important nutrients for preventing osteoporosis and helping bones reach peak bone mass. It names strength training or resistance training among the best physical activities for bone health, and says people who already have osteoporosis should check with a doctor or physical therapist before choosing exercise, since high-impact activity should be avoided.

NIAMS also advises stopping smoking and limiting alcohol to one drink a day for women and two for men. To prevent falls, which can lead to fractures, it suggests removing clutter, improving lighting, installing grab bars, and wearing supportive, non-slip shoes.

When to see a doctor

Johns Hopkins Medicine says to ask your doctor about testing if you have osteopenia, and to seek care promptly for a bone that breaks from a minor fall, a collapsed vertebra, or sudden back pain or height loss.
