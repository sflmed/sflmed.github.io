How Podiatrists and Vascular Surgeons Work Together on Limb Salvage

November 6, 2026

When a foot wound will not heal, the wound is often only part of the problem. Two things can overlap: nerve damage that lets small injuries go unnoticed, and reduced blood flow that makes healing harder. Programs that address both together are called limb salvage programs.

What limb salvage means

UChicago Medicine describes limb salvage as “a multidisciplinary program designed to identify diseased limbs (legs and feet)” and then repair or restore the blood supply, tissues or muscles to preserve function and minimize amputation. Our article on limb salvage surgery and how vascular surgeons help prevent amputation covers the SFL side in more detail.

Why a wound can need more than one kind of care

The National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK) explains that nerve damage can mean you may not feel a pebble inside your sock or a blister on your foot, which can lead to cuts and sores. It also says that “not having enough blood flowing to your legs and feet can make it hard for a sore or an infection to heal,” and that gangrene and foot ulcers that do not get better with treatment can lead to an amputation of your toe, foot, or part of your leg.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) says that if an infection does not get better with treatment, a toe, foot or leg may need to be amputated, and that early treatment greatly lowers your risk of amputation.

SFL has written about this overlap as well: why diabetic foot wounds need prompt vascular evaluation and how poor blood flow can sit behind non-healing wounds.

Who is on the team

Podiatrists and vascular surgeons both serve on the teams at university programs like these. The UCSF Center for Limb Preservation is led by a vascular surgeon and a podiatric surgeon. It pools the expertise of vascular surgeons, podiatrists, reconstructive microsurgeons and other specialists, and coordinates with specialists in infectious diseases, endocrinology, cardiology, orthopedics and rehabilitation.

The Johns Hopkins diabetic foot and wound clinic lists vascular surgeons, surgical podiatrists, endocrinologists, wound care nurses and advanced practice staff on its team, along with board-certified wound care specialists, orthopedic surgeons, infectious disease experts, physical therapists and certified orthotists. Its stated goal is healing recurrent ulceration and decreasing the rate of major amputations in people with diabetes. UChicago Medicine's limb salvage team includes vascular surgeons, a podiatrist, endocrinologists and plastic surgeons.

Our overview of diabetic vascular care describes SFL's team-based approach to screening, treating and supporting patients with diabetes.

What the care can involve

On the blood flow side, UChicago Medicine lists balloon angioplasty, which opens a narrow or blocked artery, and lower extremity bypass, in which a surgeon connects arteries above and below a blockage using a prosthetic tube or the patient's own vein. The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) describes angioplasty, stents and bypass surgery as well, and says bypass may be used to heal wounds or to save a damaged foot or leg when angioplasty is not as likely to work. UChicago also lists exercise therapy and medications as ways peripheral artery disease may be treated without surgery. You can read about angiogram, angioplasty and stenting at SFL Medical Group on our main website. For how SFL's vascular surgeons weigh the two procedures, see our article on angioplasty versus bypass surgery.

UCSF says its center focuses on preventing and treating foot ulcers, preventing their recurrence, and helping patients return to independent walking. Our page on diabetic foot ulcers describes this kind of care at SFL.

What you can do

NIDDK says that checking your feet each day helps you spot problems early, before they get worse, and suggests looking each evening, including between your toes. UChicago Medicine lists a healthy weight, exercise as a regular part of the day, smoking cessation, and daily checks of your feet and legs for sores, injuries or temperature changes among the lifestyle steps. The NHLBI adds that quitting smoking can reduce your symptoms and your risk of complications from peripheral artery disease.

When to call

NIDDK lists skin on your foot that becomes red, warm or painful, which are signs of a possible infection, and a foot infection that becomes black and smelly, a sign you might have gangrene.

The CDC says not to wait for your next appointment if you notice pain or cramping in your legs during activity, tingling, burning or pain in your feet, loss of the sense of touch or trouble feeling heat or cold, a change in the color or temperature of your feet, or a blister, sore, ulcer, infected corn or ingrown toenail.

UChicago Medicine says that foot pain at rest or sores that heal slowly are reasons to contact your doctor, and that for urgent symptoms you should call your doctor or go to the emergency room immediately.
